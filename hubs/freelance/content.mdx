## How to price your freelance work

Most pricing mistakes come from one shortcut: taking an old salary, dividing it by 2,080 working hours and charging that. A freelancer pays for their own holidays, equipment, insurance and tax, and can only bill some of the hours they work. A rate built that way is almost always too low.

A better order is to work from the bottom up.

1. **Find your floor.** The <ToolLink slug="hourly-rate-calculator">freelance hourly rate calculator</ToolLink> adds tax, business expenses and unpaid time on top of the income you want, then spreads the total over the hours you can actually bill. The result is the least you can charge and still hit your goal.
2. **Turn it into the prices clients see.** Some clients buy by the day, especially on longer contracts. The <ToolLink slug="day-rate-to-salary-calculator">day rate to salary calculator</ToolLink> shows what a day rate adds up to over a year once holidays, sick days and gaps between contracts are taken out, and what permanent salary it is really worth.
3. **Review it every year.** Costs rise and skills grow. The <ToolLink slug="rate-increase-calculator">rate increase calculator</ToolLink> shows what a higher rate does to your monthly income even if some work leaves, and how much you could lose and still earn the same.

## Hourly, daily or by the project?

Hourly pricing is simple and protects you when the scope keeps changing. Day rates suit contracts and on-site work, and stop clients counting minutes. Project prices reward you for working quickly and let clients budget with confidence.

You don't have to pick only one. Many freelancers keep a minimum hourly rate as their private floor, quote day rates for contracts, and use the hourly figure to check that every fixed-price quote still covers their time.
