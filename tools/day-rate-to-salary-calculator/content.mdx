This day rate to salary calculator shows what a contractor day rate adds up to over a year, after the days you can't bill, and roughly what permanent salary it is worth. It also works backwards: enter a salary and it finds the day rate you need to match it. All figures are before tax.

## How to use this contractor day rate calculator

1. **Choose a direction.** "Day rate to salary" starts from your rate. "Salary to day rate" starts from a job's salary.
2. **Pick your currency.** The starting values change to suit it. Anything you type is kept.
3. **Enter your day rate or the salary.** Use gross figures, before tax.
4. **Adjust your days.** Start with 260 working days (52 weeks × 5). Then set your holidays, public holidays, sick days and the gaps you expect between contracts.
5. **Set a benefits uplift.** This is your estimate of what an employee gets on top of salary, such as pension, paid leave and insurance.

The result updates as you type. The bar chart shows where your working days go, and "Compare two plans" lets you test a different rate or gap.

## How a day rate is converted to a salary

A contractor only earns on the days they bill. So the calculator first takes the unpaid days off the year, then multiplies:

```text
billable days     = working days − holidays − public holidays − sick days − gaps
annual income     = day rate × billable days
equivalent salary = annual income ÷ (1 + benefits uplift)
hourly equivalent = day rate ÷ 8
```

The last step matters. An employee on £88,000 gets paid leave, a pension and other benefits on top. Dividing by one plus the uplift turns contractor income into a salary you can compare fairly.

## Salary to day rate: what you need to match a job

If you're weighing a permanent job against contracting, work backwards. Take the salary, add the benefits uplift, then spread it over your billable days:

```text
day rate needed = salary × (1 + benefits uplift) ÷ billable days
```

With the default days and a 20% uplift, matching a £60,000 job needs **£339.62** a day. Anything below that and the permanent role is worth more, before you even think about job security.

## Contractor vs permanent salary: why day rates look so high

A £500 day rate sounds like a lot when you multiply it by 260 days. But a contractor isn't paid for holidays, public holidays, sick days or the weeks between contracts, and they pay for their own pension, equipment and training.

That's why a fair comparison uses billable days and a benefits uplift. Contracting can still pay more, but the gap is usually much smaller than the headline rate suggests. Tax rules for contractors differ by country, so check with an accountant before you compare take-home pay.

### Comparing two plans

Say the UK developer is offered a long contract at 10% less (£450 a day) with no gaps. Billable days rise to 222, so yearly income is £99,900: still £6,100 less than £500 a day with 10 days of gaps. A lower rate needs more extra days than you might expect to break even.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Count every unpaid day.** Holidays, public holidays, sick days and gaps all reduce what you earn. Leaving one out flatters the result.
- **Be honest about gaps.** If you've contracted before, look at your real gaps over the last year or two.
- **Use your own uplift.** 20% is only a starting point. A generous pension or private health cover can push it higher.
- **Compare gross with gross.** This calculator is before tax, so compare it with the gross salary of a job, not the take-home.
- **Check the hourly figure.** The hourly equivalent assumes an 8-hour day. If your days are longer, your real hourly rate is lower.
