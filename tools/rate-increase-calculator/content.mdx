This rate increase calculator shows what raising your rate does to your monthly income, even if some clients or hours leave. It also tells you the most work you could lose and still earn exactly what you earn today, so you can raise prices with numbers instead of nerves.

## How to use the calculator

1. **Pick your currency** and enter your **current hourly rate**.
2. **Enter your billable hours per month.** Use a typical month, not your busiest one.
3. **Set the rate increase** you're thinking about, from 1% to 200%.
4. **Set the work you might lose.** This is the share of hours or clients you think could leave after the increase. Be a little pessimistic.

The main result is the change in your monthly income, with the yearly change underneath. The chart shows how your income moves as you lose more work, with today's income as a flat line. Where the two cross is your break-even point.

## How the change in income is calculated

Your income is your rate times your billable hours. A rate increase raises the first number, and losing work lowers the second:

```text
new rate          = rate × (1 + increase)
income now        = rate × hours
hours after       = hours × (1 − work lost)
income after      = new rate × hours after
change            = income after − income now
break-even loss   = increase ÷ (1 + increase)
```

The break-even loss is the key number. It's the share of work you can lose and still earn the same. A 20% increase breaks even at 16.67%, not 20%, because the remaining work is now paid at the higher rate.

## How much to raise freelance rates

There's no single right increase, but there is a safe way to choose one. Pick a few increases, say 10%, 20% and 30%, and for each one ask: how much work would I really lose? If your honest guess is well below the break-even loss, the increase pays off.

Start with new clients if you're nervous. They have no old rate to compare with, so you can test a higher price with no risk to existing work.

## Will you lose clients if you raise your rates?

Some clients may leave. That's normal, and it's often the clients who pay least or argue most about price. What matters is whether the clients who stay make up for it, and that's exactly what this calculator measures.

Remember that lost work isn't only lost money. Fewer hours at a higher rate gives you time back for marketing, better clients, learning or rest. The "hours freed up" line shows how much.

## Telling clients about the new rate

Once you have picked an increase, the **Tell your clients** box writes a short notice for you. Choose the date the new rate starts and it fills in your current rate, your new rate and that date. Copy it into an email and add your client's name. Give at least a month's notice, and keep work you have already agreed at the old rate.

## Using it as a price increase calculator

The maths works for any price, not only hourly rates. Enter a product price as the rate and units sold per month as the hours, and you'll see how a price increase plays out if some customers stop buying.

## Worked examples

### 1. A US designer raising $50 to $60

Lena bills 100 hours a month at $50. She raises her rate by 20% and expects to lose 10% of her work.

- New rate: **$60.00**
- Income now: **$5,000.00** a month · after: **$5,400.00**
- Hours after the change: **90** (10 hours freed up)
- Change: **+$400.00** a month
- Break-even loss: **16.67%**

**What this means for Lena:** she earns more and works 10 fewer hours. She could lose up to one hour in six before the increase stops paying off.

### 2. An Indian developer who loses too much work

Vikram bills 120 hours a month at ₹1,000. He raises his rate by 10% to ₹1,100, but expects to lose 15% of his work.

- New rate: **₹1,100.00**
- Income now: **₹1,20,000.00** · after: **₹1,12,200.00**
- Hours after the change: **102**
- Change: **−₹7,800.00** a month
- Break-even loss: **9.09%**

**What this means for Vikram:** a 10% rise can only absorb about 9% of lost work. If he expects to lose 15%, he should either raise by more for new clients only, or keep his rate and find better clients first.

### 3. A UK coach with no client loss

Amara coaches 60 hours a month at £80. Her clients are loyal, so she raises her rate by 25% and expects to lose nobody.

- New rate: **£100.00**
- Income now: **£4,800.00** · after: **£6,000.00**
- Change: **+£1,200.00** a month
- Break-even loss: **20%**

**What this means for Amara:** even if one client in five left, she would still earn what she earns today.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Guess your losses honestly.** It's easy to expect the worst, but don't assume nobody will leave either.
- **Give notice.** A month or more in writing is fair, and it gives clients time to budget.
- **Raise for new clients first** if you're unsure. It tests your new price with no risk.
- **Count the time you get back.** Hours freed up can go into finding better-paid work.
- **Don't wait years.** Small yearly increases are easier for clients to accept than one big jump.
- **Know your floor.** Check your minimum rate with the <ToolLink slug="hourly-rate-calculator">freelance hourly rate calculator</ToolLink> before you decide how far to go.
