This break even calculator shows how many sales you need each month to cover your costs, and the revenue that takes. Add a target profit and it also shows the sales you need to reach it. It works for products, freelance projects or clients.

## How to use the calculator

1. **Pick your currency** and choose **what you sell**: units, projects or clients. This only changes the labels.
2. **Enter your fixed costs per month.** These stay the same however much you sell: rent, software, insurance, salaries.
3. **Enter your price** for one unit, project or client.
4. **Enter the variable cost of one sale.** Materials, payment fees, shipping or a contractor you pay per job.
5. **Add a target profit** if you have one. Leave it at zero to see only the break-even point.
6. **Add your current sales** if you know them, to see your margin of safety.

The main result is the number of sales you need, rounded up. The chart shows your revenue and total costs rising together, with the break-even point where they cross.

## How the break-even point is calculated

Each sale first pays for its own variable cost. What's left, the contribution, goes towards your fixed costs. Once enough sales have covered the fixed costs, every extra sale is profit.

```text
contribution       = price − variable cost
margin             = contribution ÷ price
break-even sales   = fixed costs ÷ contribution          (rounded up)
break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ margin
sales for profit   = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution   (rounded up)
```

The result is rounded up because you can't make part of a sale. If the exact answer is 50.42, you need the 51st sale to cover everything.

## How many sales to break even: units vs revenue

Break-even in sales tells you how much work or stock you need. Break-even revenue tells you how much money must come in. They describe the same point: revenue is just break-even sales times your price.

Use sales when you plan your time or stock, and revenue when you compare with your accounts or a bank statement.

## Margin of safety and price changes

Your **margin of safety** is how far your sales could fall before you stop covering your costs:

```text
margin of safety = (current sales − break-even sales) ÷ current sales
```

With a break-even of 500 sales and 600 sales today, your margin of safety is 16.67%: sales could drop by about a sixth before you make a loss. A negative figure means you are below break-even now.

The **If you change your price** table shows break-even sales at your price and 10% or 20% either side, with costs unchanged. In the default example, a 10% rise cuts break-even from 500 to 400 sales, while a 20% cut doubles it to 1,000. Small price changes move break-even a lot.

## A freelance break even calculator, too

Most break-even tools assume you sell physical products. But the same maths works for freelancers. Choose "Projects" or "Clients", put your monthly business costs in fixed costs, your price per project in price, and anything you pay out per project, such as a subcontractor or platform fee, in variable cost.

Your break-even number is the minimum number of projects or clients you need each month before you've earned a single unit of profit.

## Worked examples

### 1. An online course seller

Ola sells a course for $50. Each sale costs $30 in platform fees, hosting and support. Her fixed costs are $10,000 a month, and she wants $5,000 profit.

- Contribution per sale: **$20.00** (margin **40%**)
- Break-even: **500** sales a month
- Break-even revenue: **$25,000.00**
- Sales for $5,000 profit: **750**

**What this means for Ola:** she needs 250 more sales than break-even to reach her profit goal, so marketing has to work every month.

### 2. A freelance web designer

Tom charges £99 for a small website job. He pays a subcontractor and tools £39.50 per job, and his fixed costs are £3,000 a month.

- Contribution per job: **£59.50** (margin **60.1%**)
- Exact break-even: **50.42** projects, so **51** projects a month
- Break-even revenue: **£4,991.60**

**What this means for Tom:** 51 small jobs a month is a lot. Raising his price or adding bigger projects would cut that number fast.

### 3. A coach with monthly clients

Neha charges ₹5,000 a month per coaching client. Each client costs her ₹2,000 in tools and materials, and her fixed costs are ₹1,00,000 a month. She wants ₹50,000 profit.

- Contribution per client: **₹3,000.00** (margin **60%**)
- Exact break-even: **33.33** clients, so **34** clients
- Break-even revenue: **₹1,66,666.67**
- Clients for ₹50,000 profit: **50**

**What this means for Neha:** 34 clients cover her costs. Every client after that adds ₹3,000 to her profit.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Sort costs carefully.** If a cost goes up with each sale, it's variable. If it doesn't, it's fixed.
- **Include your own pay** in fixed costs if you want the business to pay you a salary, not just break even.
- **Watch the margin.** A low margin means you need a lot of sales. Small price rises often help more than cutting costs.
- **Recalculate when anything changes:** a new tool, a price change or a supplier increase.
- **Check your time.** If you sell your own hours, make sure you can actually deliver that many projects. The <ToolLink slug="utilization-rate-calculator">utilization rate calculator</ToolLink> helps you see how much of your time is billable.
