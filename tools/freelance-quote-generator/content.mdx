## How to use this free estimate generator for freelancers

1. **Pick your currency** and check the quote number, date and how many days the quote is valid.
2. **Add your details and the client's.** Everything is optional, so fill in only what you want on the quote.
3. **List the work as line items.** Each line has a description, a quantity or number of hours, and a rate. Add, remove or reorder lines with the buttons.
4. **Optional: add a contingency, a discount or a tax line.** The tax line starts at 0%: enter the label and rate that apply to you.
5. **Write your notes and terms:** what's included, revision rounds and payment terms.
6. **Click Download PDF** and choose **Save as PDF** in the print window. Only the quote is printed.

The preview beside the form (or the **Preview** tab on a phone) shows the quote exactly as it will print, and updates as you type.

When the quote is sent, **Start a new quote** keeps your details and terms, clears the client and the lines, and suggests the next number (Q-0001 becomes Q-0002). **Clear form** deletes everything saved on this device.

## How the quote total is worked out

```text
line amount  = quantity × rate                       (rounded to the cent)
subtotal     = sum of the line amounts
contingency  = subtotal × contingency %
discount     = (subtotal + contingency) × discount %
tax          = (subtotal + contingency − discount) × tax %
total        = subtotal + contingency − discount + tax
valid until  = issue date + days valid
```

Each amount is rounded to the cent before it's added up, so the numbers on the PDF always add up exactly.

## Project quote calculator: quote or estimate?

A **quote** is a fixed price for a defined piece of work. An **estimate** is your best guess and can change. Clients usually prefer a quote, because they know the cost before they start. If the scope isn't clear yet, quote a small first stage, such as discovery or a design concept, and quote the rest once you both know what's needed.

## Worked examples

### 1. A US web project with contingency and a discount

Leo quotes a new client for a website: 10 hours of design and 20 hours of development at $75, plus hosting setup at $120. He adds a 10% contingency for unknowns and a 5% discount because the client was referred by a past customer.

- Subtotal: **$2,370.00**
- Contingency (10%): $237.00
- Discount (5%): −$130.35
- Total: **$2,476.65**, valid for 30 days

**What this means for Leo:** the contingency is shown openly, so the client sees why the total is above the line items, and the discount is a clear thank-you for the referral.

### 2. An Indian brand-design quote with a tax line

Asha quotes a logo at ₹15,000, a brand guide at ₹8,000 and three extra revision rounds at ₹1,500 each. She adds a tax line at the 10% rate she entered for this example.

- Subtotal: **₹27,500.00**
- Tax (10%): ₹2,750.00
- Total: **₹30,250.00**

**What this means for Asha:** pricing extra revision rounds as their own line makes it clear that revisions beyond the agreed number are paid work.

This is a simple quote. If you add tax, some countries have rules on how it must be shown (for example GST in India or VAT in the UK/EU). Check your local rules.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Describe deliverables, not activities.** "Five-page website, mobile-ready" is clearer than "web work".
- **Say what's not included**, such as copywriting, photos or hosting fees.
- **Limit revision rounds** and price extra rounds as a line.
- **Always set a validity date**, so an old quote can't be accepted at an old price.
